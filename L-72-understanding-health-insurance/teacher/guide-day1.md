# Teacher Guide: Understanding Health Insurance

## Day 1 — Health Insurance Fundamentals

### Lesson Overview

This lesson introduces students to health insurance as a practical, immediately relevant financial skill—not an abstract adult concept. Students will learn the cost-sharing vocabulary (premium, deductible, copay, coinsurance, out-of-pocket maximum), compare major plan types (HMO, PPO, EPO, HDHP), understand where coverage comes from (employer, marketplace, Medicaid), and learn about the critical turning-26 coverage transition.

The lesson deliberately opens with a 22-year-old (Maya) facing her first real health insurance decision to establish relevance. By the end of class, students should be able to read a benefits summary and calculate what a medical bill would actually cost them under a given plan.

**Important Context:** Health insurance is the single area of personal finance where lack of knowledge has the most immediate and severe consequences. Students who don't understand their plan often avoid necessary care or face surprise medical bills. This lesson builds the foundational literacy that prevents those outcomes.

### Learning Objectives

By the end of this lesson, students will be able to:

1. Distinguish between key cost-sharing components of a health plan (premium, deductible, copay, coinsurance, out-of-pocket maximum)
2. Compare plan types (HMO, PPO, EPO, HDHP) and identify the tradeoffs of each
3. Explain how employer-sponsored, ACA marketplace, Medicaid/CHIP, and Medicare coverage differ
4. Describe what happens when a young adult turns 26 and ages off a parent's plan
5. Evaluate when a high-deductible plan paired with an HSA makes financial sense

### Detailed Facilitation Notes

#### Opening Hook (5 minutes)

Start with Maya's scenario from the student content. Read it aloud or display it:

> *Maya is 22, just got her first full-time job, and HR just sent her a 47-page benefits packet. She has until Friday to pick a health plan. She has no idea what any of it means, and the wrong choice could cost her thousands of dollars.*

Ask the class:
- **"How many of you have health insurance right now?"** (Most will raise hands—they're on a parent's plan.)
- **"How many of you know what a deductible is?"** (Expect 5-20% to raise hands.)
- **"How many of you will be off your parents' plan in the next 5-10 years?"** (All hands.)

This establishes the hook: You WILL be Maya. This lesson is about making sure you're not the person who guesses wrong.

#### Key Concepts: The ER Visit Math (10 minutes)

This is the most important section of the lesson. Work through the ER visit calculation step-by-step on the board:

**Plan specs:**
- $200/month premium
- $1,500 deductible
- 20% coinsurance after deductible
- $5,000 out-of-pocket maximum

**Medical event:** Broken arm, ER visit, total bill $4,200

**Calculation:**
1. "First, you hit your deductible. How much is that?" → $1,500
2. "What's left of the bill?" → $4,200 - $1,500 = $2,700
3. "Now coinsurance kicks in. You pay 20% of the remaining $2,700. What's 20% of $2,700?" → $540
4. "Total you pay for this visit?" → $1,500 + $540 = $2,040

**Critical clarifications to emphasize:**
- The $200/month premium you've been paying all year does NOT count toward the deductible
- This $2,040 DOES count toward your $5,000 OOP max
- Copays may or may not count toward the deductible (varies by plan—students need to read their plan documents)
- Preventive care (annual physical, certain screenings) is covered at 100% before the deductible under ACA plans

**Common student misconceptions that will surface here:**
- "So the deductible is what I pay every month?" → No, that's the premium.
- "Once I pay the deductible, insurance covers everything?" → No, you still pay coinsurance until you hit the OOP max.
- "If I already paid $200/month for six months, doesn't that count?" → No, premiums are separate from cost-sharing.

Address these misconceptions directly when they arise. This is formative assessment in real-time.

#### Plan Type Comparison (10 minutes)

Draw a simple 2×2 grid on the board:

```
              Low Monthly Cost | High Monthly Cost
              ________________|__________________
Low Flexibility    |            |
                  |    HMO     |      ???
                  |____________|__________________
High Flexibility  |            |
                  |    ???     |      PPO
                  |____________|__________________
```

Ask students: "Where does an HMO fall?" → Low cost, low flexibility (need referrals, must stay in-network)

Ask: "Where does a PPO fall?" → High cost, high flexibility (no referrals, can go out-of-network)

Ask: "What would go in the other two quadrants?" → EPO (high flexibility in-network but no OON coverage = moderate cost), HDHP (low cost if healthy, but high deductible = less immediate flexibility)

This visual helps students remember the tradeoffs. The key point: **There is no "best" plan type. The right choice depends on your health status, income, and preferences.**

**Discussion break (2 minutes):**
"Who in this room has a chronic condition that requires regular doctor visits or prescriptions?" (Don't force anyone to share, just raise hands anonymously if comfortable.) "For those students, would an HMO with low copays make more sense, or an HDHP with a high deductible?" → HMO or low-deductible PPO, because they'll hit the deductible anyway and need affordable access to care.

"Who rarely sees a doctor and is generally healthy?" → HDHP might be the better financial choice because you save on premiums and can build HSA tax-free savings.

#### Coverage Sources (10 minutes)

This section moves quickly. The goal is not deep expertise but a mental map of where coverage comes from.

**Employer-sponsored:** Most common for full-time workers. Employer pays most of the premium. Coverage starts 30-90 days after hire.

**ACA Marketplace (healthcare.gov):** For individuals who don't have employer coverage or whose employer plan is unaffordable. Subsidies available based on income. Open enrollment once a year.

**Medicaid:** For low-income individuals. Eligibility varies by state—check your state's rules. **Action item for students:** Find out if your state expanded Medicaid. Search "[your state name] Medicaid eligibility."

**CHIP:** For children in families that earn too much for Medicaid but can't afford private insurance.

**Medicare:** For 65+ or people with certain disabilities. Not relevant to most students now, but good to know.

**Key takeaway:** If you don't have employer coverage, **healthcare.gov** is your starting point. That's the URL to remember.

#### Real-World Example: Jordan's Job (7 minutes)

Present Jordan's three plan options (from student content Scenario 1). Have students work in pairs to calculate which plan is cheapest for Jordan's situation:
- Healthy, 23 years old, goes to doctor once a year for a checkup
- Three plans: Gold ($180/month), Silver ($95/month), Bronze HDHP ($60/month + HSA)

**Give students 3-4 minutes to calculate.** Circulate. Listen for:
- Are they remembering to add 12 months of premiums?
- Are they considering that the annual checkup is preventive care (covered at 100%)?
- Are they factoring in the HSA tax savings for Plan C?

**Bring class back together.** Walk through the answer:
- Plan A: $2,160/year in premiums + $0 out-of-pocket (checkup covered) = $2,160
- Plan C: $720/year in premiums + $0 out-of-pocket (checkup covered) = $720
- Plan C + HSA: If Jordan contributes $3,000 to HSA, saves ~$660 in taxes (22% bracket) → Net cost: $60

**Discussion:** "Why doesn't everyone choose Plan C?" → Because if you get sick or injured, you pay full price until you hit the $3,500 deductible. For a healthy person, that's a risk worth taking. For someone with asthma or diabetes, it's not.

#### Skill Builder Checklist (8 minutes)

Students complete the 5-item readiness checklist individually. This is both a learning activity and a self-assessment. Circulate and monitor:

**Look for:**
- Are students rating themselves honestly, or are they all marking "Confident" without thinking?
- For students who mark "Need to learn more," are they writing specific action steps or vague generalities?

**If you see vague action steps like "study more" or "look it up," intervene:**
- "What specific resource will you use?"
- "When will you do this?"
- "How will you know you've actually learned it?"

This skill builder is a portfolio artifact. Students will come back to it when they turn 26, start a job, or face a qualifying life event. The action steps need to be actionable.

#### Reflection Prompt Discussion (5 minutes)

Close with reflection prompt #3 from the student content:

> *"A friend tells you that health insurance is a waste of money because they're young and healthy. What would you say to them?"*

**Why this prompt matters:** This is the most common reason young adults go uninsured—they believe they don't need it. This misconception kills credit scores and financial futures.

**Sample student responses you're looking for:**
- "One ER visit costs thousands of dollars. You can't predict accidents."
- "Medical debt is the #1 cause of bankruptcy. It's not worth the risk."
- "Even if you're healthy now, you're required to have a way to enroll when you turn 26 or lose coverage. Missing that window means months uninsured."

**If students struggle with this prompt:** Share Marcus's scenario from the student content (turns 26, has 60 days to enroll, goes uninsured, breaks wrist, faces $5,000+ bill). Ask: "Was Marcus unhealthy? No. Did he need insurance? Yes."

### Differentiation Strategies

**For students with IEPs or scaffolding needs:**
- Provide a pre-filled glossary card with all 10 key terms and definitions
- For the ER visit math example, provide a step-by-step worksheet with blanks rather than open calculation
- Offer a choice between completing the Skill Builder Checklist digitally (with text-to-speech) or on paper

**For ELL students:**
- Clarify that "premium" in English means "monthly payment" in this context, not "best quality" (false cognate in Spanish/other languages)
- Provide translations of key terms if available (prima = premium, deducible = deductible, copago = copay)
- Pair ELL students with fluent English speakers for the Jordan calculation activity

**For advanced learners:**
- Extension task: Visit healthcare.gov, select your state, create a fictional person profile (age 25, income $30,000, no employer coverage), and compare actual Silver plan options. Present your recommendation to the class.
- Research question: "How does health insurance in the U.S. compare to universal healthcare systems in other countries? What are the tradeoffs?"

### Common Misconceptions to Address

1. **"My deductible is what I pay each month."**
   - Correction: That's your premium. Your deductible is what you pay before insurance starts covering costs.

2. **"Once I hit my deductible, insurance pays everything."**
   - Correction: After the deductible, you pay coinsurance (a percentage) until you hit your out-of-pocket maximum.

3. **"Preventive care always requires meeting the deductible first."**
   - Correction: Under ACA plans, annual physicals and certain screenings are covered at 100% before the deductible.

4. **"COBRA lets me keep my employer plan for free after losing a job."**
   - Correction: COBRA lets you keep the same plan, but you pay the FULL premium (what you were paying + what your employer was paying). It's often 3-4x more expensive than what you paid as an employee.

5. **"Medicaid is only for people who are unemployed."**
   - Correction: Many working adults qualify based on income, especially in Medicaid expansion states. Employment status is not the only factor.

6. **"I can enroll in health insurance anytime I want."**
   - Correction: You can only enroll during open enrollment (once a year) or during a Special Enrollment Period triggered by a qualifying life event (losing coverage, turning 26, moving, getting married, having a baby).

### Assessment Opportunities

**Formative Assessment:**
- During ER visit math: Cold-call students to solve each step. Check for understanding of deductible vs. premium.
- After plan type section: Quick poll — "Raise your hand if you would choose an HMO right now. Raise your hand if you'd choose a PPO. Raise your hand if you'd choose an HDHP." Ask 2-3 students to explain their choice.
- During Jordan example: Circulate during pair work, listen to student conversations, identify misconceptions in real-time.

**Portfolio Assessment:**
- Skill Builder Checklist: Scores range from 0-10 points. Students with scores below 5 should be encouraged to revisit key concepts.

**Summative Assessment (Day 2):**
- Station 1 cost calculation grid demonstrates ability to apply premium/deductible/coinsurance concepts
- Station 2 marketplace navigation shows understanding of subsidies and metal tiers
- Station 3 HSA decision tool shows ability to match plan type to health status
- Station 4 SEP timeline demonstrates understanding of enrollment windows

### Extension and Enrichment

**For students who finish early or want to go deeper:**
- Read a real Summary of Benefits and Coverage (SBC) document from healthcare.gov and identify: premium, deductible, OOP max, whether it's HMO/PPO/EPO, and whether preventive care is covered before the deductible.
- Calculate: "If I contribute the maximum to an HSA ($4,300) and I'm in the 22% tax bracket, how much do I save in taxes? How does this compare to the premium difference between an HDHP and a traditional PPO?"
- Research your state's Medicaid expansion status and income thresholds. Create a one-page infographic explaining who qualifies in your state.

## Teacher Support Notes

**If you're teaching this lesson for the first time:**

This lesson covers a lot of vocabulary quickly. Don't aim for perfect mastery of every term on Day 1—that's what Day 2 is for. Your goal today is to give students the mental framework (premium, deductible, coinsurance, OOP max, plan types, coverage sources, turning 26) and enough context to engage meaningfully with Day 2's applied activities.

**The single most important thing students should leave class understanding:**

**Health insurance decisions have deadlines. Open enrollment windows are fixed. Turning 26 creates a 60-day Special Enrollment Period. Missing those windows can mean months or years of uninsured status and exposure to catastrophic medical debt.**

If students walk out of your class knowing only that one thing, you've given them information that could save their financial future.

**Common teacher questions:**

**Q: Should I spend time on Medicare?**
A: 2-3 minutes maximum. Students should know it exists and that it's for 65+ or people with disabilities, but deep Medicare knowledge is not relevant to their immediate needs.

**Q: What if a student asks about a parent's specific plan and I don't know the answer?**
A: "That's a great question, and every plan is different. The best source of truth for your parent's plan is the Summary of Benefits and Coverage document. You can also call the number on the back of the insurance card."

**Q: What if students get overwhelmed by all the vocabulary?**
A: Normalize it. "This is deliberately complicated language designed by insurance companies. You're not supposed to find it easy. But you CAN learn it, and by the end of Day 2 you'll be able to calculate costs that most adults can't."

**Q: What if a student shares that their family is uninsured?**
A: Respond with care and without judgment. "There are resources available. If your family's income qualifies, Medicaid or ACA subsidies could make coverage very affordable. You can check healthcare.gov or call 1-800-318-2596." Offer to follow up privately if the student wants help navigating resources.
