# Teacher Guide: Understanding Health Insurance

## Day 2 — Health Insurance Decision-Making Learning Lab

### Lesson Overview

Day 2 shifts from theory to application. Students rotate through (or work sequentially on) four hands-on stations that simulate real health insurance decisions: calculating total annual costs under different plans, navigating the ACA marketplace for subsidies, deciding between HDHP+HSA vs. traditional plans based on health status, and mapping Special Enrollment Periods to life events.

Each station builds on Day 1 vocabulary and concepts but requires students to apply knowledge in new contexts. By the end of the lab, students will have created a personal "Coverage Timeline" artifact identifying when they'll need to make coverage decisions in the next 10 years.

**Format:** This lesson works as either a rotation model (groups rotate through stations every 15-20 minutes) or a sequential model (individuals/pairs work through all four stations at their own pace). Choose based on your classroom setup and student needs.

### Learning Objectives

By the end of this lesson, students will be able to:

1. Calculate total annual health insurance costs (premiums + out-of-pocket) under different plan scenarios
2. Navigate the ACA marketplace subsidy system and recommend appropriate metal tiers based on income
3. Evaluate when an HDHP+HSA makes financial sense vs. a traditional PPO or FSA
4. Identify which life events trigger Special Enrollment Periods and the enrollment window length
5. Create a personal coverage timeline mapping major life events to insurance decisions

## Station 1: Health Plan Cost Calculator

### Purpose
Students discover that the cheapest-premium plan is not always the cheapest plan overall. They learn to calculate total annual cost (premiums + out-of-pocket) and match plan choice to health status.

### Setup
Provide the three-plan, three-scenario grid from student day2.md. Students fill in total annual cost for each combination (9 calculations total).

**The Three Plans:**
- **Plan A (Gold PPO):** $280/month, $500 deductible, $20 copay, 10% coinsurance, $2,000 OOP max
- **Plan B (Silver PPO):** $160/month, $1,500 deductible, $35 copay, 20% coinsurance, $4,000 OOP max
- **Plan C (Bronze HDHP):** $80/month, $3,000 deductible, no copays, 30% coinsurance, $6,000 OOP max

**The Three Scenarios:**
- **Scenario A (Healthy Year):** Annual physical (free) + $350 urgent care visit
- **Scenario B (Moderate Year):** $4,830 in medical bills (doctor visits, specialist, MRI, prescriptions)
- **Scenario C (High-Need Year):** $23,500 in medical bills (appendectomy + follow-up)

### Facilitation Notes

**Common student errors you'll see:**
1. **Forgetting to add premiums:** Students calculate only out-of-pocket costs and forget that premiums are paid regardless of whether you use healthcare.
   - **Fix:** When you see this, ask: "What did this person pay every month even before they got sick?"

2. **Not understanding when the OOP max kicks in:** Students continue calculating coinsurance even after the person has hit their out-of-pocket maximum.
   - **Fix:** "Once you hit the OOP max, what happens?" → Insurance pays 100% for the rest of the year.

3. **Copay confusion:** Students don't know whether to count copays toward the deductible or separately.
   - **Fix:** "For this exercise, assume copays count separately from the deductible. In real plans, check your Summary of Benefits and Coverage—it varies."

### Answer Key (for teacher reference)

| Scenario | Plan A Total | Plan B Total | Plan C Total | Winner |
|----------|--------------|--------------|--------------|--------|
| **Healthy Year (A)** | $3,360 premium + $350 OOP = **$3,710** | $1,920 premium + $350 OOP = **$2,270** | $960 premium + $350 OOP = **$1,310** | **Plan C** |
| **Moderate Year (B)** | $3,360 premium + $500 deductible + ~$433 coinsurance = **$4,293** (hits OOP max at $2,000, so actual = **$5,360**) | $1,920 premium + $1,500 deductible + ~$666 coinsurance = **$4,086** | $960 premium + $3,000 deductible + ~$549 coinsurance = **$4,509** | **Plan B** |
| **High-Need Year (C)** | $3,360 premium + $2,000 OOP max = **$5,360** | $1,920 premium + $4,000 OOP max = **$5,920** | $960 premium + $6,000 OOP max = **$6,960** | **Plan A** |

**Key insight students should discover:** The Bronze HDHP (Plan C) wins if you're healthy. The Gold PPO (Plan A) wins if you have a major medical event. The Silver plan (Plan B) is the "middle ground" compromise.

**If students finish early:** Ask them to calculate: "At what dollar amount of medical bills does Plan A become cheaper than Plan C?" (Answer: When total bills exceed ~$7,000-$8,000, the lower OOP max of Plan A outweighs the premium savings of Plan C.)

## Station 2: ACA Marketplace Navigator

### Purpose
Students learn that income determines subsidy eligibility, that Medicaid expansion varies by state, and that metal tiers represent different cost-sharing levels.

### Setup
Provide the four profiles from student day2.md. Students answer questions about subsidy eligibility and recommend a metal tier for each person.

**The Four Profiles:**
1. **Alex:** Single, $20,000/year, no employer coverage
2. **Jordan:** Single, $35,000/year, employer plan costs 14% of income
3. **Taylor (family of 3):** $55,000/year, no employer coverage
4. **Casey:** Single, $65,000/year, employer offers affordable coverage

### Facilitation Notes

**Key teaching moments at this station:**

**Profile 1 (Alex at $20,000):**
- Alex is at 133% of FPL ($15,060 for a single adult in 2025)
- In Medicaid expansion states, Alex qualifies for Medicaid (up to 138% FPL)
- In non-expansion states, Alex likely doesn't qualify for Medicaid but DOES qualify for marketplace subsidies
- **Common student question:** "Why would a state choose not to expand Medicaid?" This is a political question. Keep the answer factual: "It's a state budget and policy decision. Some states expanded, some didn't. That's why you need to check your specific state."

**Profile 2 (Jordan at $35,000, unaffordable employer plan):**
- Employer coverage is considered "unaffordable" if the employee share exceeds 9.5% of income
- 14% > 9.5%, so Jordan CAN use the marketplace instead
- Jordan will qualify for subsidies (at 232% FPL)
- **Recommended tier:** Silver or Gold (Jordan has asthma, needs regular care—don't choose Bronze)

**Profile 3 (Taylor family at $55,000):**
- At 178% FPL for a family of 3 ($30,900 in 2025)
- Qualifies for marketplace subsidies
- Child may qualify for CHIP depending on state (CHIP typically covers up to 200-300% FPL)
- **Recommended tier:** Silver (subsidies are strongest here; cost-sharing reductions available)

**Profile 4 (Casey at $65,000, affordable employer plan):**
- At 432% FPL, Casey does NOT qualify for marketplace subsidies (cutoff is 400% FPL, though some states extend this)
- Employer plan is affordable ($180/month is well under 9.5% of $65,000)
- **Correct answer:** Take the employer plan. It will almost certainly be cheaper than an unsubsidized marketplace plan.

**Important note to emphasize:** "These rules change. The income thresholds, the subsidy levels, and even the 400% FPL cap can change based on legislation. When you're actually shopping for coverage, use healthcare.gov's calculator—it will tell you exactly what you qualify for based on current law."

**If students ask about specific states:**
- Don't try to memorize all 50 states' Medicaid status
- Instead, teach the process: "Go to healthcare.gov, enter your state and income, and it will tell you if you qualify for Medicaid or subsidies."

## Station 3: HSA vs. FSA vs. Neither Decision Tool

### Purpose
Students understand that the HDHP+HSA combination is financially optimal for healthy people but the wrong choice for people with high, predictable medical costs.

### Setup
Provide three profiles with different income and health situations. Students analyze each and recommend HDHP+HSA, traditional PPO, or PPO+FSA.

**The Three Profiles:**
1. **Riley (age 24):** Healthy, $38,000 income, sees doctor 2x/year
2. **Morgan (age 28):** Type 1 diabetes, insulin costs $400/month
3. **Drew (age 31):** Pregnant, delivery expected in 6 months

### Facilitation Notes

**Profile A (Riley):**
This is the textbook case for HDHP+HSA. Riley is healthy, has low medical expenses, and can save thousands in premiums while building tax-free HSA savings.

**Calculation guidance for students:**
- HDHP premium: $90/month × 12 = $1,080/year
- PPO premium: $190/month × 12 = $2,280/year
- Premium difference: $1,200/year
- If Riley contributes $4,300 to HSA (max for 2025), tax savings at 22% bracket = $946
- Net cost of HDHP after tax savings: $1,080 - $946 = $134/year
- Even if Riley has a $500 medical expense (pays full cost due to deductible), total cost = $634
- PPO total cost (just premiums, assuming minimal medical use): $2,280

**Winner:** HDHP+HSA by a landslide.

**Profile B (Morgan with diabetes):**
This is where the HDHP breaks down. Morgan's insulin costs $400/month = $4,800/year. Even before other medical costs, Morgan will hit the $2,500 HDHP deductible.

**Calculation guidance:**
- Under HDHP: $1,080 premium + $2,500 deductible (hit after ~6 months of insulin costs) + coinsurance on remaining costs
- Under PPO: $2,280 premium + copays for doctor visits ($30 × 4 = $120) + prescription copays ($10/month × 12 = $120) = ~$2,520/year
- **Even with the higher PPO premium, Morgan's total annual cost is lower** because the copay structure caps out-of-pocket costs more predictably than the HDHP's deductible

**Winner:** PPO (or PPO + FSA to pay prescription copays with pre-tax dollars)

**Common student misconception:** "But the HDHP has lower premiums, so it's always better for young people."
**Correction:** "Age doesn't determine your best plan—your health status does. A 22-year-old with Crohn's disease should probably choose a PPO. A 45-year-old who's healthy should probably choose an HDHP."

**Profile C (Drew, pregnant):**
Pregnancy and delivery are high-cost medical events. Drew will hit any plan's deductible and likely hit or approach the OOP max.

**Calculation guidance:**
- Delivery typically costs $10,000-$15,000 before insurance
- Under HDHP: $1,080 premium + $2,500 deductible + coinsurance until OOP max (let's say $5,000) = $6,080 total
- Under PPO: $2,280 premium + $750 deductible + coinsurance until OOP max ($3,000) = $5,280 total
- **Winner:** PPO, because the lower OOP max saves money in a year with predictable high medical costs

**Teaching moment:** "This is why open enrollment timing matters. If Drew knows she's planning to get pregnant, she should switch from HDHP to PPO during open enrollment BEFORE she gets pregnant. Once she's pregnant, that's not a qualifying event to change plans mid-year."

## Station 4: Special Enrollment Period & Life Events Checklist

### Purpose
Students learn which life events trigger coverage windows and what happens if you miss those windows.

### Setup
Students classify eight life events (triggers SEP? yes/no; window length) and work through two scenario walk-throughs (Sofia turns 26, Derek loses job).

### Facilitation Notes

**The Eight Life Events Answer Key:**

| Life Event | Triggers SEP? | Window Length |
|------------|---------------|---------------|
| Turning 26 and losing parent's coverage | **Yes** | **60 days** |
| Getting married | **Yes** | **60 days** |
| Having or adopting a child | **Yes** | **60 days** |
| Moving to a new state | **Yes** | **60 days** |
| Losing a job and employer coverage | **Yes** | **60 days** |
| Getting a raise | **No** | N/A |
| Starting a new job that offers coverage | **Yes** | **30-60 days** (depends on employer policy) |
| Voluntarily dropping coverage | **No** | N/A |

**Key teaching point:** Special Enrollment Periods are triggered by *involuntary* loss of coverage or major life changes. Voluntary actions (quitting a plan, getting a raise) don't qualify. You can't game the system by dropping coverage and re-enrolling at will.

**Scenario 1: Sofia Turns 26**

This scenario is designed to generate emotional engagement. Sofia turns 26 on April 15. She doesn't realize it's a qualifying event. She's uninsured. On June 10, she breaks her wrist.

**Questions to guide students through:**
1. **When did Sofia's SEP window open?** April 15 (her birthday)
2. **When did it close?** 60 days later = June 14
3. **On June 10, is she still in her window?** Yes—barely. She has 4 days left.
4. **What should Sofia do RIGHT NOW?** Enroll in coverage immediately (healthcare.gov or employer if available). She can still get coverage starting July 1 if she enrolls by June 14.

**What if she misses the deadline?**
- She's uninsured until next open enrollment (November-January, coverage starts January 1)
- She'll have to pay the full uninsured cost of the ER visit, X-rays, cast, and follow-up care
- Uninsured ER visit for a broken wrist: typically $3,500-$8,000

**Teaching moment:** "This is why the 60-day window matters. Sofia isn't careless or irresponsible—she just didn't know. How many of you knew before this class that turning 26 triggered a 60-day SEP?" (Expect zero hands.) "That's exactly why we're teaching this."

**Scenario 2: Derek Loses His Job**

Derek loses his job March 1. Employer coverage ends March 31. He has 60 days to enroll.

**Questions:**
1. **Deadline?** 60 days from March 31 = May 30
2. **COBRA cost: $580/month. Why so high?** Derek now pays the full premium (what he was paying + what his employer was paying). Employers typically cover 70-80% of the premium.
3. **Should Derek take COBRA or marketplace?** Depends on his income and subsidy eligibility. If he qualifies for Medicaid or a subsidized marketplace plan, those are almost certainly cheaper than COBRA.

**Teaching moment:** "COBRA is a safety net, but it's expensive. It's usually a last resort—use it if you need continuous coverage for an ongoing medical treatment and can't switch doctors. Otherwise, marketplace or Medicaid is usually the better financial choice."

**Portfolio Deliverable: My Coverage Timeline**

Students write three life events likely to happen to them in the next 10 years and what they'll need to do when each happens.

**Look for specificity:**
- **Good:** "Turning 26 → 60 days before my birthday, check if my employer offers coverage. If not, visit healthcare.gov and compare Silver plans with subsidies."
- **Vague:** "Turning 26 → get insurance"

If you see vague answers, push students: "Where will you go to get insurance? What's the first step?"

## Wrap-Up and Final Reflection (5 minutes)

Bring the class together. Ask 2-3 students to share their answer to the final reflection question:

> **"What is one thing you learned today that you think most adults your age don't know about health insurance? Why does that gap exist?"**

**Sample student responses:**
- "Most people don't know that turning 26 is a qualifying event with a 60-day window. The gap exists because nobody teaches this in school."
- "I didn't know that the cheapest premium doesn't mean the cheapest total cost. People just look at the monthly payment and don't calculate the whole year."
- "I learned that an HSA is basically a second retirement account if you don't spend it. Most people think it's use-it-or-lose-it like an FSA."

**Close with this:** "You now know more about health insurance than most 25-year-olds in America. The difference between you and them: you'll know what to do when you turn 26, start a job, or have a qualifying life event. They won't. That knowledge gap is the difference between financial security and medical debt."

## Differentiation and Support

**For students who struggle with math:**
- Provide a partially completed Station 1 grid (fill in 3-4 cells, students complete the rest)
- Pair struggling students with stronger math students for Station 1 and 3
- Allow calculator use (should be standard anyway)

**For advanced students who finish early:**
- Extension: "Visit healthcare.gov for {{STATE_NAME}}. Create a profile for a 25-year-old earning $30,000. Which Silver plan would you recommend and why?"
- Research question: "How does the HSA triple tax advantage compare to a Roth IRA? Can you use an HSA as a retirement account?"

**For ELL students:**
- Provide a glossary card with key terms translated if available
- Pair with fluent English speakers for stations
- Offer extended time to complete stations

## Common Teacher Questions

**Q: What if students don't finish all four stations?**
A: Prioritize Stations 1 and 4. Station 1 teaches cost calculation (the most practical skill). Station 4 teaches the turning-26 window (the most urgent information). Stations 2 and 3 are valuable but can be completed as homework if needed.

**Q: What if a student asks about their specific family situation (parent's plan, Medicaid status, etc.)?**
A: "Every plan and every state is different. The best thing you can do is check your specific situation on healthcare.gov or call 1-800-318-2596. I can teach you the framework, but you'll need to look up the details for your state and your family's plan."

**Q: A student says their family can't afford health insurance. What do I say?**
A: Respond with care and resources: "If your family's income qualifies, Medicaid or marketplace subsidies can make coverage very affordable—sometimes even free. Visit healthcare.gov or call the number I just mentioned. They can tell you exactly what your family qualifies for." Offer to follow up privately if the student wants help.

**Q: Should I use real healthcare.gov during class?**
A: Optional, but helpful. If you have classroom internet access, pull up healthcare.gov during Station 2 so students can see the actual interface. Walk them through: enter zip code → see available plans → compare metal tiers. It demystifies the process.

## Preparation for Future Lessons

This is the final lesson in the health insurance mini-unit. Students should now have:
- Foundational vocabulary (premium, deductible, copay, coinsurance, OOP max)
- Plan type knowledge (HMO, PPO, EPO, HDHP)
- Coverage source map (employer, marketplace, Medicaid)
- Critical timeline awareness (turning 26 = 60-day SEP)

**Next steps for students:**
- Bookmark healthcare.gov
- Save their Coverage Timeline portfolio artifact
- Set a reminder 60 days before their 26th birthday
- When starting a new job, ask HR: "When does health coverage start? What plan options are available?"

**For teachers:** Consider assigning a quick follow-up in 6 months or at the start of next semester: "Pull out your Health Insurance Readiness Checklist. Have any of your ratings changed? Have you completed any of the action steps you wrote?"

This reinforcement keeps the knowledge fresh and makes it more likely students will actually apply it when needed.
