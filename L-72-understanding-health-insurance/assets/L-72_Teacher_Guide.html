L-72: Understanding Health Insurance

Teacher Guide - Days 1 & 2

Comprehensive facilitation guide for health insurance fundamentals and decision-making lab

Day 1: Health Insurance Fundamentals

Lesson Overview

Duration: 50-60 minutes

Format: Lecture with embedded discussion, vocabulary building, skill builder activity

Core Message: Health insurance decisions have deadlines that arrive whether you're ready or not. The vocabulary and structures introduced today are the minimum literacy required to avoid costly mistakes.

Learning Objectives

By the end of Day 1, students will be able to:

  1. Distinguish between key cost-sharing components of a health plan (premium, deductible, copay, coinsurance, out-of-pocket maximum)
  2. Compare plan types (HMO, PPO, EPO, HDHP) and identify the tradeoffs of each
  3. Explain how employer-sponsored, ACA marketplace, Medicaid/CHIP, and Medicare coverage differ
  4. Describe what happens when a young adult turns 26 and ages off a parent's plan
  5. Evaluate when a high-deductible plan paired with an HSA makes financial sense

Lesson Timeline

0-5 min: Opening Story - Maya's 47-Page Panic
  • Hook students with the relatable scenario of a young adult facing health insurance choices with no preparation
  • Emphasize the deadline pressure and real financial stakes
5-20 min: Key Concepts & Cost Structure
  • Teach premium, deductible, copay, coinsurance, out-of-pocket maximum, network
  • Work through the broken arm example ($4,200 ER bill) step-by-step on the board
  • Clarify common misconceptions (premiums don't count toward deductible, preventive care is often covered before deductible)
20-30 min: Plan Types (HMO, PPO, EPO, HDHP)
  • Compare plan structures using table or chart
  • Discuss tradeoffs: cost vs. flexibility, referral requirements, network restrictions
  • When does each plan type make sense?
30-40 min: Coverage Sources & Turning 26
  • Employer-sponsored insurance, ACA marketplace, Medicaid/CHIP, Medicare
  • Emphasize the turning-26 coverage cliff and 60-day Special Enrollment Period
  • Real-world examples: Jordan's first job, Marcus turns 26
40-50 min: HSA Decision & Skill Builder
  • Explain HSA triple tax advantage and when HDHP+HSA makes sense
  • HSA vs. FSA key differences
  • Students complete Health Insurance Readiness Checklist
50-55 min: Summary & Preview Day 2
  • Recap vocabulary and key decision points
  • Preview tomorrow's Learning Lab stations

Facilitation Notes - Day 1

Opening Hook Strategy

Maya's story is designed to make health insurance feel urgent and relevant. Many students assume this topic is "for adults later." Counter this by emphasizing:

  • The turning-26 deadline affects everyone (3-8 years from now for most high schoolers)
  • First job health insurance decisions happen within weeks of hire
  • The wrong choice can cost thousands of dollars per year

Common Student Misconceptions

  1. "Health insurance is only for sick people or old people" - Counter with: Young healthy people still need coverage for accidents and emergencies. One uninsured ER visit can cost $3,000-$15,000.
  2. "Premiums count toward the deductible" - Clarify: Premiums are separate. You pay premiums every month regardless of whether you use healthcare.
  3. "Cheapest premium = cheapest plan" - Tomorrow's lab will prove this false through calculations.
  4. "I can just sign up for insurance when I need it" - Explain open enrollment windows and Special Enrollment Periods. You can't wait until you're sick.

Teaching the Cost Structure Example

When teaching the broken arm scenario ($4,200 ER bill), work through it slowly on the board:

  1. Write the plan details clearly: $200/month premium, $1,500 deductible, 20% coinsurance, $5,000 OOP max
  2. Show the $4,200 total bill
  3. Step 1: First $1,500 → You pay 100% (deductible)
  4. Step 2: Remaining $2,700 → You pay 20% coinsurance = $540
  5. Total out-of-pocket: $2,040
  6. Emphasize: This $2,040 counts toward your $5,000 OOP max. If you have another medical event later in the year, you've already paid $2,040 toward the $5,000 cap.

Ask students: "If you had another $8,000 medical event later this year, how much more would you pay?" (Answer: $2,960 to hit the $5,000 OOP max, then insurance covers 100%)

Plan Types: Make It Visual

Create a comparison table on the board or project one. For each plan type (HMO, PPO, EPO, HDHP), list:

  • Referral requirement? (Yes/No)
  • Out-of-network coverage? (Yes/No/Emergency only)
  • Typical premium level (Low/Medium/High)
  • Best for: (Brief description)

Ask students: "If you travel frequently for work, which plan type makes the most sense?" (PPO - out-of-network coverage in other states)

Critical Emphasis: Turning 26

This is the most immediately relevant deadline for students. Many will turn 26 within 3-8 years. Emphasize:

  • Coverage typically ends on your 26th birthday month (check your specific plan)
  • You have 60 days from losing coverage to enroll in a new plan
  • Missing this 60-day window means you could be uninsured for up to 10 months until the next open enrollment
  • One medical emergency without insurance can result in tens of thousands of dollars in debt

Real-world action: Set a reminder in your phone for 60 days before your 26th birthday to start researching health insurance options.

Assessment Opportunities - Day 1

  • Formative: Cold-call students to define vocabulary terms throughout the lesson
  • Formative: Ask students to work through a simplified cost calculation on their own (e.g., $1,000 medical bill with a $500 deductible and 20% coinsurance)
  • Formative: Exit ticket: "What is the most important thing to do when you turn 26?"
  • Summative: Health Insurance Readiness Checklist (completed as skill builder)

Day 2: Health Insurance Decision-Making Learning Lab

Lesson Overview

Duration: 50-60 minutes (or full block period)

Format: Four hands-on stations, small group or individual work, portfolio deliverable

Core Message: The cheapest-premium plan is not always the cheapest plan overall. Income determines subsidy eligibility. HSAs are powerful for healthy people but wrong for chronic conditions. Life events create coverage windows you can't afford to miss.

Learning Lab Objectives

By the end of Day 2, students will be able to:

  1. Calculate total annual costs under different health plans for different usage scenarios
  2. Navigate the ACA marketplace subsidy system and determine eligibility
  3. Compare HDHP+HSA vs. traditional plans for different life situations
  4. Identify Special Enrollment Period triggers and understand coverage windows

Station Rotation Schedule

Option 1: Full Block (90 minutes)

  • 5 min: Introduction and station overview
  • 20 min: Station 1 (Health Plan Cost Calculator)
  • 20 min: Station 2 (ACA Marketplace Navigator)
  • 20 min: Station 3 (HSA vs. FSA vs. Neither)
  • 15 min: Station 4 (Special Enrollment Periods & Life Events)
  • 10 min: Final reflection and summary

Option 2: Standard Period (50 minutes)

  • 5 min: Introduction
  • 10 min: Station 1
  • 10 min: Station 2
  • 10 min: Station 3
  • 10 min: Station 4
  • 5 min: Reflection

Note: For standard period, students should focus on key calculations and skip extended reflection questions. Assign full worksheets as homework if needed.

Station Facilitation Guide

Station 1: Health Plan Cost Calculator

Purpose: Prove that the cheapest premium doesn't always mean the cheapest total cost.

Facilitation Tips:

  • Students often struggle with coinsurance calculations. Remind them: "After the deductible, you pay a percentage of the remaining bill."
  • Emphasize that out-of-pocket maximum is the safety net - you never pay more than this amount in a year (for covered services).
  • Walk through Scenario A (Healthy Year) together as a class before releasing students to calculate Scenarios B and C independently.

Expected Answers:

  • Scenario A (Healthy Year): Plan C wins (lowest total cost due to low premiums and minimal healthcare use)
  • Scenario B (Moderate Year): Plan B or A wins (depends on specific calculations, but lower deductible plans perform better)
  • Scenario C (High-Need Year): Plan A wins (hits OOP max quickly, then everything is covered)

Common Student Errors:

  • Forgetting to add annual premiums to out-of-pocket costs
  • Not recognizing when out-of-pocket maximum is reached
  • Confusing copay with coinsurance

Station 2: ACA Marketplace Navigator

Purpose: Understand that income determines subsidy eligibility and metal tier choice matters.

Facilitation Tips:

  • Provide calculators or allow phone calculator use for FPL percentage calculations
  • Formula: (Income / FPL for household size) × 100 = % of FPL
  • Example: Alex at $20,000 / $15,060 × 100 = 133% of FPL
  • Emphasize: Subsidies maximize at the Silver tier level

Expected Answers:

  • Alex ($20,000): 133% FPL, qualifies for marketplace subsidies, qualifies for Medicaid in expansion states, recommended Silver tier (subsidies maximize here)
  • Jordan ($35,000): 232% FPL, employer coverage is NOT affordable (14% > 9.5%), can use marketplace instead, recommended Gold tier (needs regular prescriptions for asthma)
  • Taylor ($55,000 family of 3): 178% FPL, qualifies for marketplace subsidies, child likely qualifies for CHIP in most states, recommended Silver tier
  • Casey ($65,000): 432% FPL, does NOT qualify for marketplace subsidies, should take employer coverage, recommended Bronze or Silver tier (healthy, low use)

Critical Point: Employer coverage is only considered "affordable" under ACA rules if the employee's share is less than 9.5% of income. Jordan's $420/month on $35,000 income = 14.4%, so it's NOT affordable, and Jordan can use the marketplace instead.

Station 3: HSA vs. FSA vs. Neither Decision Tool

Purpose: Understand when HDHP+HSA makes sense vs. traditional plans.

Facilitation Tips:

  • Emphasize the triple tax advantage of HSAs: pre-tax contributions, tax-free growth, tax-free withdrawals for medical expenses
  • Key insight: HSAs are wealth-building tools for healthy people, but wrong for people with chronic conditions who will hit their deductible quickly
  • FSAs are use-it-or-lose-it; HSAs roll over indefinitely - this is a major difference

Expected Recommendations:

  • Riley (healthy, 24): HDHP+HSA. Premium savings + HSA tax benefits far outweigh the higher deductible for someone who rarely uses healthcare.
  • Morgan (Type 1 diabetes): PPO. Will hit the $2,500 deductible in 6-7 months just from insulin costs alone. Lower deductible PPO with copays is much better for predictable, high medical expenses.
  • Drew (pregnant): PPO. Pregnancy and delivery will easily exceed any deductible. The lower OOP max on the PPO ($3,000 vs. likely higher on HDHP) saves money in a high-medical-use year.

Common Misconception: Students may think "high deductible is always cheaper because of lower premiums." Use Morgan's profile to show this is false for people with chronic conditions or predictable high medical needs.

Station 4: Special Enrollment Period & Life Events Checklist

Purpose: Understand that life events create coverage windows, and missing them has consequences.

Facilitation Tips:

  • Most qualifying life events trigger a 60-day Special Enrollment Period
  • NOT all life changes trigger SEP - getting a raise does not; voluntarily dropping coverage does not
  • COBRA is expensive ($400-$700/month) because you pay the full premium including what the employer was paying
  • Marketplace subsidies can make coverage much more affordable than COBRA for unemployed individuals

Life Events That Trigger SEP (60 days):

  • Turning 26 and losing parent's coverage
  • Getting married
  • Having or adopting a child
  • Moving to a new state
  • Losing a job and employer coverage
  • Starting a new job that offers coverage

Life Events That DO NOT Trigger SEP:

  • Getting a raise
  • Voluntarily dropping coverage

Sofia's Scenario: She turned 26 on April 15. Her SEP opened April 15 and closed 60 days later (around June 14). On June 10, she is still BARELY within her SEP window. If she enrolls immediately, she can get coverage. If she waits past June 14, she's stuck uninsured until the next open enrollment (November). An uninsured ER visit with X-rays and cast typically costs $3,000-$8,000.

Derek's Scenario: Deadline is May 30 (60 days from March 31). COBRA is expensive because he's paying the full premium (employer was subsidizing ~70-80% before). At $0 income while unemployed, he likely qualifies for Medicaid in expansion states. If not, marketplace Silver plan with maximum subsidy is much cheaper than COBRA. Going uninsured is extremely risky - one medical event could cost tens of thousands.

Common Questions & Answers

Q: "What if I can't afford any health insurance?"

A: Check Medicaid eligibility first (especially in expansion states). If you don't qualify for Medicaid, check if you qualify for marketplace subsidies. Premium tax credits can bring costs down to $0-$150/month for low-income individuals. Some states also have additional subsidies beyond federal levels.

Q: "Why is health insurance so expensive in the U.S.?"

A: Complex question with many factors: administrative costs, high provider prices, expensive prescription drugs, profit margins for private insurers, lack of price negotiation compared to single-payer systems. This is a good springboard for deeper policy discussion if time allows.

Q: "Can I just pay the penalty for not having insurance?"

A: The federal individual mandate penalty was reduced to $0 in 2019. However, some states (CA, MA, NJ, RI, VT, DC) have their own penalties. More importantly, being uninsured means you're one medical emergency away from tens of thousands of dollars in debt. The penalty was never the main reason to have insurance - financial protection is.

Q: "What if I get sick and THEN try to buy insurance?"

A: You can only enroll during open enrollment (November-January) or during a Special Enrollment Period triggered by a qualifying life event. You cannot buy insurance after you're already sick and get coverage for that illness unless you're in an enrollment window. Pre-existing conditions are covered under ACA, but you must enroll during a valid enrollment period.

State-Specific Customization

Important: This curriculum uses general federal rules (ACA marketplace, Medicaid expansion at 138% FPL, etc.). Your state may have specific variations:

  • Medicaid Expansion: As of 2025, 40 states + DC have expanded Medicaid. In non-expansion states, Medicaid eligibility is much more restrictive (often only for pregnant women, children, disabled individuals, or extremely low-income parents).
  • State Exchanges: Some states run their own exchanges (e.g., California's Covered California, New York State of Health) with different enrollment periods and subsidy structures.
  • CHIP Income Limits: Vary by state, typically 200-300% FPL for children.
  • State Mandate Penalties: CA, MA, NJ, RI, VT, and DC have individual mandate penalties for not having insurance.

Action Item: Before teaching, verify your state's current Medicaid expansion status, CHIP thresholds, and whether your state has its own exchange or uses healthcare.gov.

Follow-Up & Homework Options

  • Reflection Essay: "Write a 1-page letter to your 25-year-old self explaining what you need to know about health insurance before you turn 26."
  • Real-World Research: Visit healthcare.gov and find three Bronze, Silver, and Gold plans available in your county. Compare their costs for a 26-year-old individual.
  • Family Interview: Ask a parent or guardian how they chose their current health plan. What factors did they consider? What would they do differently?
  • Case Study Analysis: Provide additional profiles and ask students to recommend plan type and explain why.

Resources for Teachers

  • healthcare.gov: Official ACA marketplace (federal and state exchanges)
  • KFF (Kaiser Family Foundation): Excellent health policy research and state-by-state data
  • Medicaid.gov: State-by-state Medicaid information
  • NAIC (National Association of Insurance Commissioners): Consumer guides to health insurance
  • IRS Publication 969: Official HSA rules and contribution limits

Final Notes

This lesson is designed to be practical and immediately applicable. Many students will face these exact decisions within 3-8 years. The goal is not to make them health insurance experts - it's to ensure they know:

  1. The vocabulary needed to read a benefits packet
  2. The deadlines that matter (turning 26, open enrollment, Special Enrollment Periods)
  3. Where to go for help (healthcare.gov, employer HR, Medicaid office)
  4. How to compare plans based on their health needs and financial situation

Success Metric: If every student leaves this lesson knowing to set a reminder for 60 days before their 26th birthday to research health insurance options, you've succeeded.
