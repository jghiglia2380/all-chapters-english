# Chapter: Understanding Health Insurance

## Day 1: Health Insurance Fundamentals

### Overview

When most people hear "estate planning," they picture elderly millionaires meeting with lawyers in mahogany-paneled offices. But when Maya, age 22, got her first full-time job, HR sent her a 47-page benefits packet. She had until Friday to pick a health plan. She had no idea what any of it meant, and the wrong choice could cost her thousands of dollars.

Health insurance is uniquely complicated because it's simultaneously insurance, a payment system, and a network contract. Unlike car insurance where you pay a premium and hope you never use it, health insurance is something most people use regularly—which means the cost structure matters every time you see a doctor.

This chapter introduces the mechanics of health insurance cost-sharing, the major plan types, where coverage comes from, and what happens at age 26 when you age off a parent's plan. The goal isn't to make you a health insurance expert—it's to make sure you're never the person who says "I didn't know I had to do anything" when the deadline arrives.

### Learning Objectives

By the end of this lesson, you will be able to:

1. Distinguish between key cost-sharing components of a health plan (premium, deductible, copay, coinsurance, out-of-pocket maximum)
2. Compare plan types (HMO, PPO, EPO, HDHP) and identify the tradeoffs of each
3. Explain how employer-sponsored, ACA marketplace, Medicaid/CHIP, and Medicare coverage differ
4. Describe what happens when a young adult turns 26 and ages off a parent's plan
5. Evaluate when a high-deductible plan paired with an HSA makes financial sense

### Key Concepts

**Premium** — The monthly cost you pay regardless of whether you use healthcare, like a subscription fee. This payment does not count toward your deductible.

**Deductible** — The amount you pay out of pocket before insurance starts covering costs. Important: some services like preventive care (annual physicals, certain screenings) are covered at 100% before you meet your deductible under most ACA plans.

**Copay** — A fixed dollar amount you pay per visit or prescription. Example: $25 to see your primary care doctor, $50 for urgent care, $10 for a generic prescription.

**Coinsurance** — After meeting your deductible, the percentage split between you and the insurer. An 80/20 plan means the insurer pays 80% and you pay 20% of covered costs.

**Out-of-Pocket Maximum (OOP Max)** — The most you'll pay in a plan year. After hitting this limit, the insurer covers 100% of covered services for the rest of the year.

**Network** — The group of doctors and hospitals that have contracts with your insurer. In-network providers cost significantly less than out-of-network providers. Some plans don't cover out-of-network care at all.

**HMO (Health Maintenance Organization)** — Requires a primary care physician (PCP) and referrals to see specialists. Lower cost but less flexibility. No out-of-network coverage except emergencies.

**PPO (Preferred Provider Organization)** — No referral needed to see specialists, can see any doctor, but in-network costs less than out-of-network. Higher premiums but more flexibility.

**HDHP (High Deductible Health Plan)** — Lower monthly premiums, higher deductible (typically $1,600+ for individuals). Often paired with an HSA for tax savings.

**HSA (Health Savings Account)** — Tax-advantaged account available only with HDHPs. Contributions are pre-tax, growth is tax-free, and withdrawals for qualified medical expenses are tax-free—a triple tax advantage. Unlike FSAs, HSA funds roll over every year indefinitely.

### Deeper Exploration

#### How the Cost Structure Actually Works Together

Let's work through a concrete example. You have a plan with:
- $200/month premium
- $1,500 deductible
- $30 copay for primary care visits
- 20% coinsurance after deductible
- $5,000 out-of-pocket maximum

You break your arm and go to the ER. The total bill is $4,200. Here's what you pay:

1. **First $1,500:** You pay 100% (your deductible)
2. **Remaining $2,700:** You pay 20% coinsurance = $540
3. **Total out-of-pocket for this visit:** $2,040

Important clarifications:
- The $200/month premium you've been paying all year doesn't count toward the deductible
- Your $30 copays for routine doctor visits may or may not count toward the deductible (varies by plan)
- This $2,040 DOES count toward your $5,000 OOP max
- If you have another medical event costing $8,000 later this year, you'd only pay $2,960 more (the remaining gap to hit your $5,000 OOP max), then insurance covers 100%

This is why reading your plan documents matters—different plans handle copays and preventive care differently.

#### Plan Types and When Each Makes Sense

**HMO:** Best for people who have a regular doctor and predictable health needs. You must choose a primary care physician who coordinates all your care and provides referrals to specialists. Lower cost but less flexibility. If you see a specialist without a referral, you pay the full cost. Good choice if you value lower premiums and don't mind the coordination requirement.

**PPO:** More flexibility—no need for referrals, you can see any doctor you want. In-network providers cost less, but you still get some coverage for out-of-network care (usually at a higher cost-sharing percentage like 40% instead of 20%). Higher premiums than HMOs. Best for people who want provider choice or who travel frequently and need access to care in different areas.

**EPO (Exclusive Provider Organization):** Hybrid model—no referrals needed like a PPO, but absolutely no out-of-network coverage like an HMO (except emergencies). Less common than HMO/PPO but becoming more popular. Good middle ground on cost.

**HDHP:** Right for healthy people who rarely use healthcare and want to build HSA savings. Wrong choice for someone with a chronic condition who has frequent medical needs. The high deductible means you pay full price for most care until you hit the threshold, which can be financially difficult if you need regular prescriptions or specialist visits.

#### Where Coverage Comes From

**Employer-Sponsored Insurance:** The most common path for people with full-time jobs. The employer typically pays 70-80% of the premium, and the employee pays the rest through pre-tax payroll deductions. Coverage usually starts 30-90 days after hire. Family members (spouse, children) can often be added for an additional premium.

**ACA Marketplace (healthcare.gov):** Individual plans purchased through the federal or state marketplace. Subsidies (premium tax credits) are available based on income for people earning between 100-400% of the federal poverty level. Open enrollment happens once per year, usually November through January. Outside open enrollment, you can only enroll if you experience a qualifying life event that triggers a Special Enrollment Period.

**Medicaid:** Government program for low-income individuals and families. Eligibility varies significantly by state. In states that expanded Medicaid under the ACA, adults with income up to 138% of the federal poverty level may qualify. In states that didn't expand, eligibility is much more restrictive. No premiums or very low premiums, minimal cost-sharing.

**CHIP (Children's Health Insurance Program):** For children in families that earn too much for Medicaid but can't afford private insurance. Income thresholds vary by state but generally cover families earning up to 200-300% of the federal poverty level.

**Medicare:** Federal program for people age 65+ or those with certain disabilities. Not relevant to most high school students now, but important to understand the basics: Part A covers hospital stays, Part B covers doctor visits and outpatient care, Part D covers prescriptions, and Medicare Advantage (Part C) is an alternative that bundles these through private insurers.

#### Turning 26: The Coverage Cliff

Under the Affordable Care Act, young adults can stay on a parent's health plan until their 26th birthday. When you turn 26, coverage typically ends on your birthday month (some plans extend to the end of that month—check your plan documents).

This triggers a **Special Enrollment Period** — you have 60 days from losing coverage to enroll in a new plan. Miss that window and you may go uninsured until the next open enrollment period, which could be 10 months away.

**What to do when you're approaching 26:**

1. **60 days before your birthday:** Check if your employer offers health insurance and when you're eligible
2. **If your employer doesn't offer insurance or you're not employed:** Check if your income qualifies you for Medicaid in your state
3. **Visit healthcare.gov:** See what marketplace plans are available and if you qualify for subsidies
4. **Compare costs:** Calculate total annual cost (premiums + expected out-of-pocket) for each option
5. **Enroll before the deadline:** Missing the 60-day window is not a neutral choice—one ER visit or surgery without insurance can result in tens of thousands of dollars in medical debt

#### The HSA Decision

A Health Savings Account is only available if you have a High Deductible Health Plan. The tax advantages are powerful:

- **Contributions are pre-tax:** If you earn $40,000 and contribute $3,000 to an HSA, your taxable income drops to $37,000
- **Growth is tax-free:** Any interest or investment gains in the HSA are not taxed
- **Withdrawals for medical expenses are tax-free:** This is a triple tax advantage that no other account offers

**Key differences from an FSA (Flexible Spending Account):**
- FSA funds are use-it-or-lose-it each year; HSA funds roll over indefinitely
- FSA contribution limits are lower ($3,200 in 2025); HSA limits are higher ($4,300 individual, $8,550 family in 2025)
- FSAs are available with any health plan; HSAs only work with HDHPs

**When an HDHP + HSA makes sense:**
For a healthy 22-year-old who rarely goes to the doctor, an HDHP with HSA is often the financially optimal choice. You save thousands in premiums, contribute the savings to your HSA tax-free, and the HSA becomes a secondary retirement account (after age 65, you can withdraw HSA funds for any purpose penalty-free, though non-medical withdrawals are taxed as income like a traditional IRA).

**When it doesn't make sense:**
For someone with asthma who needs daily medication, a specialist visit every quarter, and occasional urgent care visits, the math often favors a lower-deductible PPO or HMO even with higher premiums. You'll hit the deductible quickly anyway, so the premium savings from an HDHP don't outweigh the immediate cash-flow burden of paying full price for prescriptions and care until the deductible is met.

### Implications and Importance

Health insurance is where financial illiteracy has its most acute consequences. Medical debt is the leading cause of bankruptcy in the United States. Students who don't understand their plan often avoid care when they need it (because they don't know they're covered) or get blindsided by bills they didn't expect (because they used an out-of-network provider).

The vocabulary in this chapter—premium, deductible, copay, coinsurance, out-of-pocket maximum, network—is the minimum literacy required to read a benefits packet without guessing. The plan types and coverage sources give you a map of where coverage can come from and what the tradeoffs look like.

The most important thing you can take from this chapter: **health insurance decisions have a deadline.** Open enrollment windows are fixed. The turning-26 clock runs whether or not you're paying attention. The students who end up with medical debt they can't afford are often not the ones who couldn't afford insurance—they're the ones who didn't know they needed to act.

### Real-World Examples

#### Scenario 1: Jordan's First Job

Jordan, 23, starts a job at a marketing company. HR offers three plans:

- **Plan A (Gold):** $180/month premium, $500 deductible, $25 copay
- **Plan B (Silver):** $95/month premium, $2,000 deductible, $40 copay
- **Plan C (Bronze HDHP):** $60/month premium, $3,500 deductible, no copays, eligible for HSA

Jordan is healthy, goes to the doctor once a year for a checkup, and takes no regular medications.

**The math:**
- **Plan A:** $2,160/year in premiums. Annual checkup is covered as preventive care (no cost). If Jordan stays healthy all year, total cost: $2,160
- **Plan C:** $720/year in premiums. Annual checkup covered. Even if Jordan has a $500 urgent care visit (pays full cost due to deductible), total cost: $1,220
- **Plan C + HSA:** Jordan contributes $3,000 to HSA tax-free (saves ~$660 in taxes at 22% bracket). Net cost after tax savings: $560

Even with an unexpected medical expense, Jordan comes out ahead with Plan C. The HSA also builds tax-free savings for future medical costs.

#### Scenario 2: Marcus Turns 26

Marcus has been on his mom's insurance since birth. He turns 26 in March, works part-time at a coffee shop earning $28,000/year. His employer doesn't offer insurance. He has 60 days from his birthday to enroll somewhere or he'll be uninsured.

**His options:**
1. **Check Medicaid eligibility:** At $28,000/year, he's at 215% of the federal poverty level ($13,590 for an individual in 2025). In Medicaid expansion states, he would NOT qualify (cutoff is 138% FPL). In non-expansion states, he likely wouldn't qualify either. He needs to check his specific state.

2. **Healthcare.gov marketplace:** At $28,000 income, Marcus qualifies for a premium tax credit. A Silver plan that would normally cost $400/month might cost him only $150/month after subsidies. He needs to apply during his Special Enrollment Period.

3. **Do nothing:** One ER visit uninsured costs $3,000-$15,000. A surgery could cost $30,000-$100,000. Hospitals will bill him, send to collections, and damage his credit. "I didn't know" is not a defense.

Marcus's action plan: Visit healthcare.gov within 10 days of his birthday, compare Silver plans with subsidies, enroll before day 60.
