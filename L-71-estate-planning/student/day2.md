## Learning Lab Activities

### Activity 1: Will vs. No Will Simulation (15 minutes)
**Objective:** Experience the consequences of dying with and without a will through interactive scenarios

**Instructions:**
1. You'll analyze three scenarios showing what happens to estates with and without proper planning:
   - **Scenario A**: Single person, age 26, $180,000 in assets, serious partner, no will
   - **Scenario B**: Married couple, age 32, two young children, $450,000 estate, no guardians designated
   - **Scenario C**: Recently divorced parent, age 38, $275,000 estate, will still names ex-spouse

2. For each scenario, the simulator shows two outcomes:
   - **Without Will**: State intestacy laws determine distribution; court appoints administrator and guardians
   - **With Will**: Person's stated wishes control distribution; designated executor and guardians take roles

3. Track specific consequences:
   - Asset distribution: Who gets what?
   - Timeline: How long until resolution?
   - Costs: Legal fees, court costs, probate expenses
   - Family impact: Conflict, uncertainty, unintended outcomes
   - Guardian situation: Who raises minor children?

4. Calculate financial impact:
   - Probate costs as percentage of estate
   - Attorney fees and court costs
   - Delays in asset distribution
   - Lost value due to forced asset sales

5. Reflection questions:
   - Which scenario's intestacy outcome surprised you most?
   - How might unintended inheritances harm family relationships?
   - What's the hidden cost of leaving these decisions to courts?
   - At what life stage does a will become absolutely essential?

### Activity 2: Trust Comparison Tool (15 minutes)
**Objective:** Understand when trusts are appropriate and compare different trust structures

**Instructions:**
1. Analyze four family situations to determine whether a trust makes sense:
   - **Situation 1**: Young couple, $300K life insurance, children ages 2 and 4
   - **Situation 2**: Single professional, $850K estate, no children, elderly parents
   - **Situation 3**: Blended family, $1.2M estate, children from previous marriages
   - **Situation 4**: Recent graduate, $45K in assets, student loans, no dependents

2. For each situation, evaluate:
   - **Probate avoidance value**: How much would avoiding probate save in time and money?
   - **Control needs**: Does the situation require controlling when/how assets are distributed?
   - **Complexity**: Is the family situation straightforward or complicated?
   - **Cost-benefit analysis**: Do trust benefits justify setup costs ($1,500-$4,000)?

3. Compare trust types for applicable scenarios:
   - **Revocable Living Trust**: Probate avoidance, privacy, flexibility
   - **Testamentary Trust** (in will): Activates at death, controls distribution to minors
   - **Irrevocable Life Insurance Trust**: Removes life insurance from taxable estate
   - **Special Needs Trust**: Protects government benefits for disabled beneficiaries

4. Run cost comparisons:
   - Trust setup cost vs. estimated probate cost
   - Ongoing trust administration vs. executor fees
   - Time savings: Trust distribution (weeks) vs. probate (months to years)
   - Privacy value: Trust (private) vs. probate (public record)

5. Make recommendations:
   - Which situations need trusts immediately?
   - Which can wait until circumstances change?
   - What triggers should prompt trust creation?
   - Which trust type best serves each family?

**Key Learning Point**: Trusts aren't just for the wealthy—young parents with life insurance and minor children often benefit from trust planning more than retirees with large estates but adult children.

### Activity 3: Power of Attorney & Healthcare Directive Builder (10 minutes)
**Objective:** Create a personal plan for incapacity protection documents

**Instructions:**
1. Healthcare Directive Planning:
   - **Healthcare proxy selection**: Who do you trust to make medical decisions aligned with your values?
   - **Treatment preferences**: Life support decisions, organ donation wishes, quality of life priorities
   - **Communication**: What conversations should you have with your healthcare proxy before a crisis?
   - **Documentation**: Where will you store the original? Who gets copies (doctor, hospital, family)?

2. Financial Power of Attorney Planning:
   - **Agent selection**: Who is trustworthy, responsible, and financially capable?
   - **Scope of authority**: Immediate (durable) or only upon incapacity (springing)?
   - **Specific powers**: Bill payment, investment management, tax filing, property transactions?
   - **Safeguards**: Should you require two agents to act jointly for major decisions?
   - **Successor agents**: Who serves if your first choice cannot?

3. Compare scenarios with and without these documents:
   - **Scenario A**: 28-year-old in coma after car accident
     - *With documents*: Named healthcare proxy makes decisions quickly; named agent pays bills from patient's accounts
     - *Without documents*: Family battles over treatment; conservatorship petition costs $5,000 and takes 3 months while bills go unpaid

   - **Scenario B**: 34-year-old stroke victim, unmarried with partner
     - *With documents*: Partner has legal authority to manage finances and make medical decisions
     - *Without documents*: Partner has no legal standing; estranged parents make all decisions

4. Create your personal selection criteria:
   - List potential agents for healthcare and financial POAs
   - Rate each on trustworthiness, capability, availability
   - Identify backup agents
   - Note important conversations you need to have

5. Action planning:
   - When will you create these documents?
   - Will you use an attorney, online service, or state forms?
   - Who needs to receive copies once completed?
   - How will you store originals securely but accessibly?

### Activity 4: Beneficiary Designation Audit (15 minutes)
**Objective:** Understand how beneficiary designations override wills and ensure proper coordination

**Instructions:**
1. Review the concept of beneficiary designations:
   - Accounts that bypass wills: 401(k), IRA, Roth IRA, life insurance, POD bank accounts, TOD investment accounts
   - Beneficiary forms trump wills—designations control regardless of will provisions
   - Primary beneficiaries receive assets first; contingent beneficiaries only if primary is deceased
   - Proper designations avoid probate; improper designations create unintended outcomes

2. Analyze four common beneficiary mistakes using case studies:
   - **Case 1**: Never updated after divorce—ex-spouse gets $400K life insurance despite remarriage
   - **Case 2**: Named minor children directly—court custodianship until 18, then lump sum to teenager
   - **Case 3**: Named "my estate"—forced into probate, defeating the purpose of beneficiary designation
   - **Case 4**: No contingent beneficiaries—primary beneficiary predeceased, assets go to estate and probate

3. For each case, calculate the consequences:
   - Who actually receives the assets?
   - What legal process is required?
   - What costs are incurred (probate, custodianship, legal fees)?
   - What family conflict results?
   - How could proper designations have prevented this outcome?

4. Practice proper beneficiary planning for sample situations:
   - **Situation A**: Single person, age 25, $15,000 in 401(k), dating but not engaged
     - *Recommendation*: Primary = parents; Contingent = sibling

   - **Situation B**: Married couple, ages 30/32, two young children, $200K combined retirement accounts
     - *Recommendation*: Primary = spouse; Contingent = trust for children (not children directly)

   - **Situation C**: Recently divorced parent, age 37, $180K in retirement accounts and $500K life insurance
     - *Immediate action*: Change from ex-spouse to trust for children

   - **Situation D**: Engaged couple, age 28, $50K in various accounts
     - *Recommendation*: Update after marriage; for now, parents or siblings

5. Create your personal beneficiary audit checklist:
   - List all accounts with beneficiary designations
   - Document current primary and contingent beneficiaries
   - Identify accounts needing updates
   - Note life events triggering beneficiary reviews (marriage, divorce, births, deaths)
   - Set annual review reminder

6. Common beneficiary strategy considerations:
   - **Spouses**: Usually primary beneficiary for retirement accounts (tax benefits)
   - **Minor children**: Should designate trust, not children directly
   - **Parents/siblings**: Appropriate for young singles without spouses/children
   - **Charities**: Can be contingent beneficiaries if all family members predecease you
   - **Coordination**: Ensure beneficiary designations align with overall estate plan

## Key Takeaways
- Estate planning isn't optional or only for the elderly—incapacity and unexpected death can happen at any age
- Healthcare directives and financial POAs protect you while living; wills and trusts protect your family after death
- Beneficiary designations trump wills and are often the most important part of your estate plan
- Parents of minor children need guardianship designations immediately—this should not be delayed
- The cost of basic estate planning ($100-$500) is trivial compared to the cost of inadequate planning (thousands in probate, family conflict, unintended outcomes)
- State-specific rules in {{STATE_NAME}} affect intestacy distribution, probate procedures, and estate taxes
- Document location matters—families can't use documents they can't find in emergencies
- Regular reviews are essential—estate plans should be updated after marriages, divorces, births, deaths, and significant asset changes
- Conversations matter as much as documents—discuss wishes with named agents, guardians, and family members before crises occur
