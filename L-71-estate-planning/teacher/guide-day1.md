# L-71: Introduction to Estate Planning - Teacher Guide (Day 1)

## Lesson Overview
This lesson introduces students to essential estate planning concepts and documents, emphasizing that estate planning is not just for the wealthy or elderly. Students will learn about wills, trusts, beneficiary designations, powers of attorney, healthcare directives, and guardianship—understanding how these instruments protect individuals and families during incapacity and after death. The lesson makes estate planning personally relevant to 17-year-olds by focusing on protection, not just wealth transfer.

## Learning Objectives
By the end of this lesson, students will be able to:
- Define essential estate planning documents and their purposes
- Explain the probate process and its costs/timeline
- Differentiate between dying with a will versus dying intestate
- Understand how beneficiary designations interact with wills
- Recognize the importance of powers of attorney for incapacity protection
- Evaluate guardianship designations for minor children

## Detailed Lesson Plan

### Hook & Introduction (5 minutes)
**Opening Scenario:**
"Maya, 27, was critically injured in an accident. Her fiancé Jordan knew her wishes, but without a healthcare directive, Maya's estranged parents made all medical decisions—decisions contradicting what Maya would have wanted. Meanwhile, no one could access Maya's bank accounts to pay her rent because she hadn't designated a financial power of attorney. This is what estate planning prevents."

**Facilitation:**
- Read Maya's scenario (Day 1 content, Real-World Examples section)
- Ask students: "Why did this happen? What document would have prevented each problem?"
- Address the misconception: "Who thinks estate planning is only for old, wealthy people?"
- Reveal the truth: "Estate planning is about protection and control, not just wealth. Incapacity doesn't discriminate by age."
- Preview learning outcomes: "Today we learn the documents that protect you and your family when you can't speak for yourself."

### Key Concepts (10 minutes)
**Delivery Method:** Direct instruction with visual aids

**Core Vocabulary:**
1. **Will (Last Will and Testament)**
   - Legal document directing asset distribution after death
   - Names guardians for minor children
   - Designates executor to settle affairs
   - Only effective upon death—doesn't protect during incapacity
   - **STATE NOTE**: In {{STATE_NAME}}, wills must be witnessed and signed according to state law

2. **Intestate Succession**
   - What happens when you die without a will
   - State law determines who inherits using rigid formulas
   - Rarely aligns with individual preferences
   - Court appoints administrator and guardians
   - **STATE NOTE**: {{STATE_NAME}} intestacy laws prioritize [typical state succession order]

3. **Probate**
   - Court-supervised process of validating will, paying debts, distributing assets
   - Public process (anyone can access records)
   - Costly (typically 3-7% of estate value)
   - Time-consuming (6 months to 2+ years)
   - **STATE NOTE**: In {{STATE_NAME}}, probate costs and timelines vary by county but typically [state-specific details]

4. **Trust**
   - Legal arrangement: trustee holds assets for beneficiaries
   - Avoids probate (primary advantage for most people)
   - Can control distribution timing and conditions
   - Revocable = can change; Irrevocable = permanent
   - **Example**: Parents with young children use trusts so life insurance doesn't go directly to minors

5. **Beneficiary Designation**
   - Form naming who receives specific accounts directly
   - Trumps wills—designations override any will provision
   - Common for: retirement accounts, life insurance, bank POD accounts
   - **Critical point**: Many people's largest assets pass by beneficiary designation, not will

6. **Power of Attorney (POA)**
   - Financial POA: Authorizes someone to handle money/property if you're incapacitated
   - Healthcare POA/Proxy: Authorizes someone to make medical decisions
   - Durable = remains effective during incapacity (critical)
   - Without POA: Family must seek court-appointed conservatorship (expensive, slow)

**Check for Understanding:**
- Quick quiz: "True or False: If you die without a will, your spouse gets everything." (False—depends on state law and whether you have children, parents, etc.)
- Application: "If your will says your sister gets your car, but your car's transfer-on-death form names your brother, who gets the car?" (Brother—designation trumps will)
- Scenario: "Why would a 25-year-old need a healthcare directive?" (Accidents, sudden illness—incapacity can happen at any age)

### Wills vs. Intestacy Comparison (15 minutes)
**Activity Structure:** Visual comparison with student participation

**Setup:**
Create a comparison chart showing two paths: Death WITH Will vs. Death WITHOUT Will

| Factor | WITH WILL | WITHOUT WILL (Intestate) |
|--------|-----------|--------------------------|
| Asset distribution | According to your wishes | State formula (may not match preferences) |
| Executor/Administrator | Person you choose | Court appoints someone |
| Guardians for children | People you designate | Court decides |
| Process | Probate (public, but directed by will) | Probate (public, state law controls) |
| Timeline | 6-18 months typical | Often longer due to complexity |
| Family input | Your wishes documented | Court determines |
| Partners/fiancés | Can inherit if you specify | Get nothing (not legal spouses) |
| Control | Maximum | Zero |

**Facilitation:**
1. Present Marcus's story (Real-World Examples, Day 1 content):
   - 29-year-old software engineer
   - 4-year relationship with Emma (planned to marry)
   - Dies suddenly without a will
   - Parents inherit everything; Emma gets nothing
   - **Result**: His girlfriend of 4 years loses home, security, future

2. **Class Discussion**:
   - "What surprised you about intestacy laws?"
   - "Why don't unmarried partners inherit?"
   - "What would a simple will have cost versus the outcome?" ($300 will vs. Emma losing everything)

3. **State-Specific Application**:
   - "In {{STATE_NAME}}, if you die without a will and have a spouse and children, here's what happens: [explain state formula]"
   - "If you have no spouse or children: [explain next in line per state law]"
   - "This is why planning matters—the state formula rarely matches what people actually want."

4. **Guardian Designation Emphasis** (Critical for this age group):
   - "For parents, naming guardians is the most important part of a will."
   - "Without designation, courts decide who raises your children."
   - "Family members may fight for custody, traumatizing children during grief."
   - **Example**: "Ashley and Mark died in a car accident. Their will named Mark's sister as guardian. Without this, Ashley's mother and Mark's sister would have fought in court for custody of their 3-year-old daughter—she would have been in foster care during the legal battle."

**Differentiation:**
- **Advanced**: Discuss intestacy percentages (spouse gets 50%, children split 50%, etc.)
- **Support**: Focus on the simple principle: will = your choice; no will = state's choice
- **Extension**: Research your state's specific intestacy laws and create distribution flowchart

### Trusts & Beneficiary Designations (10 minutes)
**Objective:** Understand probate avoidance and coordination

**Trusts Overview:**
- **Purpose**: Avoid probate, control distribution, privacy
- **Revocable Living Trust**: Most common for probate avoidance
  - You create during life, transfer assets in
  - You typically serve as your own trustee (full control)
  - At death, successor trustee distributes per trust terms—no court involvement
  - **Cost**: $1,500-$4,000 to set up
  - **Savings**: Avoids probate costs of 3-7% of estate + time savings

- **When trusts make sense**:
  - Parents with minor children (control how funds are used)
  - Larger estates wanting privacy
  - Complex family situations (blended families)
  - Special needs family members
  - **Not necessary for simple situations with small estates**

**Beneficiary Designations:**
- **The Trump Card**: Designations override wills
- **Common mistake**: Careful will created, beneficiaries never updated
- **Case Study**: Present example:
  - "John creates will leaving everything to his wife Sarah."
  - "His $500,000 401(k) still lists his ex-wife from 10 years ago as beneficiary."
  - "When John dies, wife Sarah inherits house ($200K) per will, but ex-wife gets retirement account ($500K) per beneficiary designation."
  - **Lesson**: Review beneficiaries annually, especially after life changes

**Beneficiary Best Practices:**
1. Review all designations annually
2. Update immediately after marriage, divorce, births, deaths
3. Name contingent beneficiaries (if primary dies first)
4. For minor children: Name trust as beneficiary, not child directly
5. Never name "my estate" (defeats probate avoidance purpose)
6. Coordinate with overall estate plan

**Class Activity:**
- Students identify all account types requiring beneficiaries:
  - 401(k), 403(b), 457 (employer retirement plans) ✓
  - IRA, Roth IRA (individual retirement accounts) ✓
  - Life insurance policies ✓
  - Payable-on-Death (POD) bank accounts ✓
  - Transfer-on-Death (TOD) brokerage accounts ✓
  - Annuities ✓
- Discuss: "How many of these do young adults in their 20s typically have?" (Often several, especially if they have employer retirement plans)

### Powers of Attorney (10 minutes)
**Objective:** Understand incapacity protection

**Two Types of POA:**

1. **Financial Power of Attorney**
   - Authorizes someone to handle money, property, business
   - Can pay bills, file taxes, manage investments, sell property
   - **Durable** (remains effective during incapacity) is essential
   - **Springing** (activates only upon incapacity) sounds good but proving incapacity is difficult
   - **Cost**: Typically $100-300
   - **Without it**: Conservatorship petition costs $5,000+ and takes months

2. **Healthcare Power of Attorney / Healthcare Proxy**
   - Authorizes someone to make medical decisions
   - Works with Living Will / Advance Directive (specifies treatment preferences)
   - Critical for life support decisions, treatment choices, end-of-life care
   - **Cost**: Often free through hospital/state forms, or $100-200 from attorney
   - **Without it**: Family may battle over treatment; doctors may not know who can decide

**Real-World Application:**
Present scenario:
"Alex, 24, had a serious motorcycle accident. He's unconscious and needs emergency surgery. His partner of 3 years has no legal standing to consent because they're not married. Alex's estranged father, who hasn't spoken to Alex in 5 years, is legal next-of-kin and makes decisions Alex would never have wanted. A simple healthcare directive naming his partner would have prevented this."

**Selection Considerations:**
- **Who to choose**: Trustworthy, responsible, willing to honor your wishes
- **Financial POA**: Choose someone financially capable
- **Healthcare POA**: Choose someone who will honor your treatment preferences even if difficult
- **Can be different people**: Your financially savvy sibling for money; your spouse for medical
- **Critical**: Discuss your wishes with them BEFORE crisis

**Discussion Prompts:**
- "Who would you trust to make medical decisions for you?"
- "Who would you trust with your bank accounts and finances?"
- "What happens if you can't communicate for months—how do bills get paid?"
- "Is 'trust' enough, or do specific skills matter for these roles?"

## Common Misconceptions

**Misconception 1:** "Estate planning is only for old people."
- **Correction:** Incapacity from accidents/illness can happen at any age. Healthcare directives and POAs protect 25-year-olds as much as 75-year-olds. Young parents need guardianship designations immediately.

**Misconception 2:** "If I don't have much money, I don't need estate planning."
- **Correction:** Estate planning is about control and protection, not wealth. A $30,000 estate creates probate costs and family conflict just like a $3 million estate. Parents with modest assets still need guardianship designations.

**Misconception 3:** "My spouse/family will automatically get everything."
- **Correction:** Intestacy laws have formulas that often surprise people. Spouses don't always get 100%. Unmarried partners get nothing. Children from prior relationships may get portions. The law's defaults rarely match preferences.

**Misconception 4:** "A will avoids probate."
- **Correction:** Wills go through probate—they direct the probate process but don't avoid it. Only beneficiary designations, trusts, and joint ownership avoid probate.

**Misconception 5:** "Trusts are only for rich people."
- **Correction:** Young parents with life insurance often need trusts to protect minor children from receiving lump sums at 18. Trusts control distribution, not just avoid taxes.

**Misconception 6:** "I can just tell my family my wishes—I don't need documents."
- **Correction:** Without legal documents, hospitals and courts won't honor oral statements. Family members' recollections may differ. Legal documents provide enforceable authority.

## Instructor Support Notes

**Time Management:**
- If running long, shorten Real-World Examples to 3 minutes (read only Chen family scenario)
- If running short, extend Powers of Attorney discussion with more scenarios
- Priority sections: Key Concepts, Wills vs. Intestacy, POAs—don't skip these

**Common Questions:**
- **"Can I write my own will?"** Yes, but requirements vary by state. Handwritten (holographic) wills are valid in some states but not others. Online services ($100-300) or attorneys ($500-2,000) ensure proper execution.
- **"Do I need a lawyer?"** For simple situations, online services often sufficient. Complex situations (blended families, large estates, business ownership, special needs) benefit from attorney expertise.
- **"What if I can't afford an attorney?"** State bar associations often provide free forms and instructions. Some areas have free legal clinics. Online services like LegalZoom, Nolo, and Willing provide affordable options ($100-300).
- **"When should I create these documents?"** Healthcare directive and financial POA: as soon as you're 18. Will: when you have assets, serious relationships, or dependents. Trusts: when complexity or estate size justifies cost.

**Sensitive Topics:**
This lesson addresses death, incapacity, and family tragedy. Students may have personal experiences with these topics:
- Acknowledge that some students may have lost family members or experienced incapacity situations
- Frame content around protection and care, not fear
- Offer alternative examples if certain scenarios seem to affect students
- Provide resources for students dealing with grief or family crises
- Partner with school counselor if students need support

## Final Notes for Teachers

**Cultural Sensitivity:**
Estate planning practices and attitudes toward death vary across cultures. Some cultures have strong preferences about who should serve as guardians or how assets should be distributed. Some religions have specific end-of-life treatment requirements. Honor these perspectives while teaching the legal framework.

**Age-Appropriate Framing:**
While this topic involves death and incapacity, frame it around protection and care rather than morbidity. Young adults respond well to empowerment framing: "These documents give YOU control rather than leaving it to courts and state laws."

**Actionability:**
Students often feel overwhelmed by complex legal topics. Emphasize actionable steps:
- At 18: Create healthcare directive and financial POA
- When serious relationship/assets: Create basic will
- When children arrive: Update will with guardians and consider trust
- Annual review: Update beneficiaries and ensure documents remain current

**Real-World Connection:**
Use local examples when possible. If your community has experienced a young person's unexpected death or incapacity, mention (respectfully) how estate planning would have helped. If local attorneys offer free workshops, invite them or direct students to those resources.
