# L-71: Introduction to Estate Planning - Teacher Guide (Day 2)

## Lesson Overview
Day 2 provides hands-on application of estate planning concepts through four interactive activities. Students analyze the consequences of dying with versus without a will, compare trust structures for different family situations, plan their own incapacity protection documents, and audit beneficiary designations. The focus is practical application—students make real planning decisions and see concrete outcomes of choices.

## Learning Objectives
By the end of this lesson, students will be able to:
- Analyze specific outcomes of intestacy versus will-directed estate distribution
- Evaluate when trusts are cost-effective versus unnecessary
- Design personal power of attorney and healthcare directive plans
- Identify and correct common beneficiary designation mistakes
- Create a personal estate planning action plan appropriate for their life stage

## Detailed Activity Plans

### Activity 1: Will vs. No Will Simulation (15 minutes)
**Setup:** Students use interactive simulator to compare estate outcomes

**Learning Objectives:**
- Visualize specific consequences of intestacy versus will-directed distribution
- Calculate financial impact of probate and administration costs
- Understand timeline differences and family conflict potential

**Facilitation:**

**Introduction (2 minutes):**
- "Yesterday we learned WHAT wills do. Today we see the actual consequences of having or not having one."
- "You'll analyze three real-life scenarios showing both paths—with a will and without."

**Scenario Analysis (10 minutes):**
Students work through three scenarios individually or in pairs:

**Scenario A: Single Person, Age 26**
- Assets: $180,000 ($80K retirement, $50K investment account, $50K car/possessions)
- Situation: Serious partner (Emma), dating 3 years, planning to marry
- No will

*With Will Outcome:*
- Emma receives all assets as designated in will
- Executor (Emma's brother) handles probate: ~$7,000 cost, 8 months
- Emma receives assets within 9 months

*Without Will Outcome:*
- Intestacy: All assets to parents (Emma gets $0)
- Court-appointed administrator: ~$9,000 cost, 12-18 months
- Parents receive assets; Emma must move from shared apartment
- Emma loses engagement ring, furniture, all shared possessions

**Scenario B: Married Couple with Children**
- Both age 32, two children (ages 2 and 4)
- Assets: $450,000 ($200K life insurance each, $50K home equity)
- No guardian designation in will

*With Will (Guardians Named) Outcome:*
- Designated guardians (sister and brother-in-law) take custody immediately
- Life insurance to trust for children's benefit
- Children's lives disrupted minimally; remain with familiar family

*Without Will (No Guardians) Outcome:*
- Children temporarily placed in foster care while relatives petition for custody
- Court battle between maternal grandmother and paternal aunt: 6 months, $25,000 legal fees
- Children traumatized by uncertainty and temporary placements
- Life insurance distributed to children's court-appointed custodian (grandmother wins), but no trust structure for management

**Scenario C: Recently Divorced Parent**
- Age 38, one child (age 7)
- Assets: $275,000 ($180K retirement, $50K home equity, $45K savings)
- Will still names ex-spouse from 8 years ago

*Updated Will Outcome:*
- Current will directs assets to child via trust
- Named guardian (brother) raises child
- Assets managed by trustee for child's benefit

*Outdated Will Outcome:*
- Ex-spouse receives everything per old will
- Ex-spouse has no legal obligation to provide for child
- Guardian (brother) has custody but no financial resources
- Child's college fund effectively goes to ex-spouse

**Student Tasks:**
For each scenario, students identify:
1. Who receives assets in each outcome
2. Total costs (probate, administration, legal battles)
3. Timeline to resolution
4. Emotional/practical impact on family
5. Cost of creating proper documents vs. cost of lack of planning

**Wrap-Up Discussion (3 minutes):**
- "Which outcome surprised you most?"
- "What's the real cost—money or family impact?"
- "At what age/situation does a will become non-negotiable?"
- "What excuse for delaying estate planning is worth these outcomes?"

**Differentiation:**
- **Support**: Provide pre-filled comparison tables; focus on just one scenario
- **Advanced**: Calculate opportunity cost of legal fees if invested instead
- **Extension**: Research actual {{STATE_NAME}} intestacy distribution percentages

### Activity 2: Trust Comparison Tool (15 minutes)
**Setup:** Students analyze four family situations to determine trust appropriateness

**Learning Objectives:**
- Evaluate cost-benefit of trusts for specific situations
- Differentiate revocable, testamentary, and special-purpose trusts
- Understand that trusts aren't only for wealthy families

**Facilitation:**

**Introduction (2 minutes):**
- "Trusts sound complicated and expensive—but for many families, they're essential and cost-effective."
- "Today you'll determine: Who needs a trust? Who doesn't? Which type?"

**Situation Analysis (10 minutes):**

**Situation 1: Young Couple with Life Insurance and Small Children**
- Ages 29 and 30, children ages 2 and 4
- Assets: $300K life insurance (each), $40K retirement, $30K savings
- Question: Need a trust?

*Analysis:*
- **Probate avoidance value**: Moderate—life insurance avoids probate via beneficiary designation, but retirement/savings don't
- **Control need**: HIGH—cannot name children directly as life insurance beneficiaries (minors can't own assets)
- **Options**:
  - Option A: Testamentary trust in will (trust activates at death, holds insurance proceeds for children)
  - Option B: Revocable living trust (holds all assets, avoids probate entirely)
- **Recommendation**: Testamentary trust minimum; revocable trust if want to avoid probate on all assets
- **Cost vs. benefit**: Testamentary trust adds $500 to will cost; prevents lump sum to 18-year-olds and provides professional management

**Situation 2: Single Professional, No Children**
- Age 41, never married, no children
- Assets: $850,000 ($450K retirement, $250K investment account, $150K home equity)
- Elderly parents
- Question: Need a trust?

*Analysis:*
- **Probate avoidance value**: HIGH—estate over $850K, probate costs would be ~$25,000-$60,000 (3-7%)
- **Control need**: Moderate—wants parents cared for, then remainder to charity
- **Privacy value**: Moderate—professional reputation/privacy concerns
- **Options**:
  - Will only: $25K-60K probate cost, public process
  - Revocable living trust: $3,000 setup, ~$1,000 annual maintenance, private, no probate
- **Recommendation**: Revocable living trust—probate cost savings justify setup expense
- **Cost vs. benefit**: $3K setup saves $25K-60K probate; privacy bonus

**Situation 3: Blended Family**
- Ages 48 and 52, married 5 years (second marriage for both)
- Combined assets: $1.2M
- His children: 2 (ages 20, 22); Her children: 1 (age 17)
- Question: Need a trust?

*Analysis:*
- **Complexity**: VERY HIGH—competing interests between current spouse and children from prior marriages
- **Control need**: CRITICAL—wants to provide for spouse during lifetime, but ensure assets go to own children ultimately
- **Options**:
  - Will only: Spouse inherits all, no guarantee children receive anything
  - QTIP Trust (Qualified Terminable Interest Property): Spouse receives income for life; principal to children at spouse's death
- **Recommendation**: QTIP or similar marital trust—only way to achieve both goals
- **Cost vs. benefit**: $4,000-7,000 setup cost prevents family battles and ensures wishes honored

**Situation 4: Recent Graduate**
- Age 23, single, no children
- Assets: $45,000 ($15K car, $10K retirement, $20K savings)
- Student loan debt: $35,000
- Question: Need a trust?

*Analysis:*
- **Probate avoidance value**: LOW—small estate, probate costs minimal
- **Control need**: LOW—straightforward situation, no special circumstances
- **Complexity**: LOW—no dependents, no special needs
- **Recommendation**: NO TRUST NEEDED—simple will sufficient
- **Priority**: Healthcare directive and POA more important than will at this stage
- **Cost vs. benefit**: Trust setup ($3,000) unjustified for $45K estate

**Student Tasks:**
For each situation:
1. Identify key factors (estate size, family complexity, control needs)
2. Calculate estimated probate costs if no trust
3. Compare trust setup costs to probate savings
4. Recommend trust type (if any) and justify
5. Identify what triggers should prompt trust creation

**Wrap-Up Discussion (3 minutes):**
- "What surprised you about who needs trusts?"
- "Is it estate size or family situation that matters more?"
- "When is a trust worth the expense? When isn't it?"

**Differentiation:**
- **Support**: Provide decision tree flowchart for trust determination
- **Advanced**: Research QTIP trusts and special needs trusts in depth
- **Extension**: Calculate 30-year cost comparison (trust setup/maintenance vs. probate costs for estate growth scenarios)

### Activity 3: Power of Attorney & Healthcare Directive Builder (10 minutes)
**Setup:** Students create personal plans for incapacity protection

**Learning Objectives:**
- Select appropriate agents for financial and healthcare decisions
- Understand the difference between scenarios with and without these documents
- Create personal action plan for obtaining these documents

**Facilitation:**

**Introduction (1 minute):**
- "These documents protect you while you're ALIVE but can't speak for yourself."
- "They're often more important than wills because incapacity is more likely than unexpected death for young adults."

**Personal Planning (6 minutes):**

**Healthcare Directive Planning:**
Students work through decision framework:

1. **Healthcare Proxy Selection:**
   - Who do you trust to honor your values and wishes?
   - Who would make difficult decisions (life support, treatment options)?
   - Who is emotionally strong enough to advocate for you?
   - Potential candidates: Parents, spouse/partner, siblings, close friends
   - **Class discussion**: "Can you name your partner if not married?" (Yes—that's the point)

2. **Treatment Preferences to Discuss:**
   - Organ donation wishes
   - Life support preferences (if permanently unconscious, would you want machines keeping you alive?)
   - Pain management priorities
   - Religious/cultural treatment considerations

3. **Documentation Plan:**
   - Where to obtain form: Hospital, state health department, attorney, online service
   - Who receives copies: Healthcare proxy, doctor, hospital file, parents/family
   - Storage location: With other important documents, accessible to proxy

**Financial Power of Attorney Planning:**
Students work through decision framework:

1. **Agent Selection:**
   - Who is financially responsible and trustworthy?
   - Who understands your financial situation?
   - Who could handle: bill payment, bank access, investment decisions, tax filing?
   - Potential candidates: Parents, financially savvy sibling, spouse/partner
   - **Consider**: Should you require two agents acting jointly for large decisions?

2. **Scope of Authority:**
   - When effective: Immediately (durable) or only upon incapacity (springing)?
   - Which powers: All financial matters or limited scope?
   - Limitations: Any transactions you want to exclude?

3. **Successor Agents:**
   - Who serves if your first choice cannot?
   - Have you discussed with both agents?

**Scenario Comparison (2 minutes):**
Present two scenarios side-by-side:

**Scenario: 28-Year-Old in Coma After Car Accident**

*With Documents:*
- Healthcare proxy makes medical decisions in consultation with doctors
- Financial agent pays rent, car payment, insurance, bills from patient's accounts
- Family focuses on patient's recovery, not legal battles
- Patient's wishes honored

*Without Documents:*
- Family fights over medical decisions (parents vs. partner)
- Hospital won't release information to partner (not legal family)
- Bills go unpaid—credit damaged, potential eviction
- Family must petition for conservatorship: $5,000+ cost, 2-3 months
- By the time conservatorship granted, damage done

**Wrap-Up (1 minute):**
- "What's the cost of these documents?" ($100-300 total)
- "What's the cost of NOT having them?" (Thousands in legal fees, family conflict, potential financial disaster)
- "Who will complete these within 30 days?" (Show of hands—create accountability)

**Differentiation:**
- **Support**: Provide template conversation guide for discussing wishes with potential agents
- **Advanced**: Research springing vs. durable POA pros/cons in detail
- **Extension**: Obtain actual state forms and complete them (with parent/guardian review)

### Activity 4: Beneficiary Designation Audit (15 minutes)
**Setup:** Students analyze beneficiary mistakes and practice proper designation

**Learning Objectives:**
- Understand that beneficiaries override wills
- Identify common beneficiary mistakes
- Create personal beneficiary review checklist

**Facilitation:**

**Introduction (2 minutes):**
- "For most young adults, beneficiary designations are more important than wills."
- "Why? Because your largest assets—retirement accounts and life insurance—pass by designation, not will."
- "A perfect will is worthless if your beneficiaries are wrong."

**Case Study Analysis (8 minutes):**
Students analyze four real cases of beneficiary mistakes:

**Case 1: The Forgotten Ex-Spouse**
- Brad and Jennifer divorced 8 years ago
- Brad remarried Angela 5 years ago
- Brad's $400K life insurance still lists Jennifer as beneficiary
- Brad dies unexpectedly
- **Outcome**: Jennifer (ex-wife) gets $400K; Angela (current wife) gets $0
- **Lesson**: Update beneficiaries immediately after divorce
- **{{STATE_NAME}} note**: Some states automatically revoke ex-spouses as beneficiaries, but don't rely on this—update explicitly

**Case 2: Minor Children Named Directly**
- Parent names 8-year-old child as direct beneficiary of $250K life insurance
- Parent dies when child is 10
- **Outcome**: Court-appointed custodian manages funds until child turns 18, then lump sum
- Child receives $250K at age 18 with no oversight
- **Better approach**: Name trust as beneficiary with terms controlling distribution (e.g., 1/3 at 25, 1/3 at 30, 1/3 at 35)

**Case 3: "My Estate" as Beneficiary**
- Person names "my estate" as beneficiary of $180K retirement account
- Goal was to follow will's distribution
- **Outcome**: Retirement account goes through probate (defeats the purpose)
- Probate costs: ~$7,000
- Public process
- Tax inefficiencies (inherited IRA tax benefits lost)
- **Better approach**: Name specific individuals/trust directly

**Case 4: No Contingent Beneficiaries**
- Person names mother as primary beneficiary on all accounts
- Mother dies before person
- No contingent beneficiaries listed
- **Outcome**: All accounts default to estate and probate
- **Better approach**: Always name contingent beneficiaries (backup if primary predeceases you)

**Practice Beneficiary Planning (4 minutes):**
Students determine appropriate beneficiaries for sample situations:

**Situation A: Single, Age 25, $15K in 401(k)**
- Current status: Never designated beneficiaries
- **Recommendation**: Primary = Parents (or siblings); Contingent = Charity or other family
- **Rationale**: Simple situation, no dependents, standard succession

**Situation B: Married, Ages 30/32, Two Young Children**
- Combined retirement: $200K
- Life insurance: $250K each
- **Recommendation**:
  - Retirement accounts: Primary = Spouse; Contingent = Trust for children
  - Life insurance: Primary = Trust for children (spouse has own insurance)
- **Rationale**: Spouse-to-spouse for retirement (tax benefits); trust for children (management and controlled distribution)

**Situation C: Recently Divorced Parent, Age 37**
- $180K retirement
- $500K life insurance
- 10-year-old child
- **Immediate action needed**: Change from ex-spouse to trust for child
- **Recommendation**: Primary = Trust for child; Contingent = Child's guardian or grandparents
- **Rationale**: Protect child's financial security; ensure proper management

**Wrap-Up (1 minute):**
- "When should you review beneficiaries?" (Annually and after major life events)
- "What life events trigger beneficiary reviews?" (Marriage, divorce, births, deaths, large asset changes)
- "Who will audit their beneficiaries this month?" (Create accountability)

**Differentiation:**
- **Support**: Provide beneficiary designation checklist template
- **Advanced**: Research inherited IRA rules and tax implications of beneficiary choices
- **Extension**: Create multi-generational beneficiary strategy (primary, contingent, remainder)

## Monitoring & Assessment Strategies

**During Activities:**
- Circulate to check understanding
- Ask probing questions: "Why did you choose that guardian/agent?" "What happens if your primary beneficiary predeceases you?"
- Look for common misconceptions (trusts always expensive, wills avoid probate, oral wishes count)
- Identify students struggling with technical concepts vs. decision-making

**Formative Assessment:**
- Activity completion check: Did students complete all scenarios?
- Decision justification: Can students explain their choices?
- Application questions: "In {{STATE_NAME}}, what happens if you die without a will and have no spouse or children?"

**Summative Assessment:**
- Personal action plan quality: Does it reflect their actual life stage? Is it realistic?
- Cost-benefit analysis accuracy: Can they weigh setup costs against protection benefits?
- Beneficiary audit completeness: Did they identify all designation-required accounts?

## Differentiation Strategies

**For Students with Family Estate Planning Experience:**
- Share insights if comfortable (but don't require)
- Analyze what worked well and what could improve in family's plan
- Consider more complex scenarios (blended families, special needs, business ownership)

**For Students Uncomfortable with Death/Incapacity Topics:**
- Frame as empowerment rather than morbidity
- Focus on protection and control
- Allow alternate examples if specific scenarios are triggering
- Provide counselor referral if needed

**For Students from Non-Traditional Families:**
- Emphasize that estate planning is MORE important when legal relationships aren't automatically recognized
- Discuss domestic partner considerations
- Acknowledge chosen family as valid designees
- Highlight need for explicit documentation

**For Advanced Students:**
- Explore special needs trusts for disabled beneficiaries
- Research generation-skipping transfer tax
- Analyze {{STATE_NAME}} estate tax planning strategies
- Investigate charitable remainder trusts

## Common Issues & Solutions

**Issue:** "This feels too personal/private to discuss."
- **Solution**: Emphasize that planning is about hypotheticals, not sharing family details. Students can complete activities without disclosing specific choices.

**Issue:** "I'm only 17—I don't need to think about this yet."
- **Solution**: Healthcare directive and POA become relevant at 18. Planning ahead shows maturity. Waiting until crisis is too late.

**Issue:** "My parents will handle everything."
- **Solution**: At 18, parents have no automatic authority. Without documents, courts decide. Being an adult means having adult protections.

**Issue:** Students paralyzed by guardian selection.
- **Solution**: "Perfect is the enemy of good." Choosing someone is better than choosing no one. You can always update.

## Reflection & Closure (5 minutes)

**Guided Reflection:**
1. **Key insight**: "What's one thing you learned today that changed your thinking about estate planning?"
2. **Personal application**: "Which document is most critical for your current life stage?"
3. **Action commitment**: "What specific step will you take in the next 30 days?"

**Class Sharing:**
- Invite volunteers to share insights (not personal details)
- Create accountability: "Who will complete healthcare directive by [specific date]?"
- Emphasize: "These documents are gifts to your family—providing clarity when they need it most."

**Final Message:**
"Estate planning isn't about being old or rich—it's about being responsible and caring. Whether you're 18 or 80, these documents protect you and the people you love. The cost is modest; the value is priceless. Start with healthcare directive and POA this month. Add a will when you have assets or dependents. Update as life changes. Your future self—and your family—will thank you."
